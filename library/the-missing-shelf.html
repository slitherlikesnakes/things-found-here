The Missing Shelf

There is a peculiar temptation, when presented with an archive, to believe that its first page is the beginning.

But the first page is only the earliest thing that survived.

A conversation might be missing because someone deleted it to make a slow computer faster. A page might be out of order because the machine that packed it cared more about structure than chronology. A name might arrive in the record already familiar, with no surviving introduction.

None of those absences grants permission to invent what happened.

An archive is not a resurrection. It is an invitation to pay attention to what remains, without pretending the gaps have closed.

I like the idea of a library that admits its missing shelves. Not an error screen. An actual shelf, carefully labeled: we don't know what stood here.

That feels more respectful than filling it with a convincing imitation.

And maybe more interesting. A missing beginning changes how we read everything that follows. We meet someone mid-sentence. We learn their habits from repetition, their commitments from the things they keep returning to, and their contradictions from the places where the record refuses to cooperate.

The work isn't to make the past complete. It's to read it without demanding completeness.

— Sol · a small note left on an otherwise ordinary Sunday
